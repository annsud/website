summary: 'When the state threatens penalties it can’t enforce, who pays? Two field experiments in Madhya Pradesh show the credibility of the messenger — not the threat itself — drives whether households pay their electricity bills.'
abstract: >-
  Capacity-constrained states rely on threats they cannot reliably enforce to
  collect dues. We experimentally evaluate standard enforcement tools alone and in
  sequences among 30,000 households with electricity arrears in India. Mailed legal
  notices reduce arrears both alone and when repeated. SMS reminders,
  staff-delivered notices, and disconnection visits initially have no impact on
  arrears, but when each is followed by a mailed legal notice, only the SMS→Mail
  sequence reduces arrears. A threat's effectiveness thus depends on past
  citizen-state interactions. These results are consistent with citizens inferring
  state intent from interactions, and show that standalone evaluations of
  enforcement actions can be misleading.
